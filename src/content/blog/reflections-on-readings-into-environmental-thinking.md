*A reflection written for the ETH Zürich course [Readings into Environmental Thinking](https://ethz.ch/content/dam/ethz/special-interest/usys/ites/ecosystem-management-dam/documents/EducationDOC/Readings_DOC/Environmental%20Readers%20Booklet%20(2022).pdf).*

## 1. Educating oneself to beauty through nature

The cold winter wind has dried my lips and reddened my face from all the walking here among the mountains of home. Now I sit on the high plateau behind my parents' house, which connects Marischio to Ceresola. I think many who have lived and still live in the valley have never climbed up here to enjoy the silence and the view. It remains a little-visited place and perhaps for this reason still intact, holding a purer essence compared to other, better-known spots. The winter sun still illuminates the meadows, and the warm light of this December afternoon contrasts with the blue sky, cloudless, clear, and fresh. A few maritime pines remain standing after the last fires, their green still standing out among the small brown oaks planted to replace the old forest. They have been growing here for six years now, but they are still too young to cover the meadows.

I remember, as a child, there was a fire that started from the train tracks running beneath the mountain. Some sparks released from the train's friction found the dryness of summer, giving rise to a great fire that soon engulfed the entire southern slope of the mountain, reaching as far as where I now sit, on the mountain's plateau. Grass was the first to return after the ashes, forming this dense meadow, thick as the hair of someone in the prime of their youth, where I now lie, welcomed by its softness. Where the trees once stood, small craters remain, giving the impression of walking through a field after a bombing. These small depressions have become shelters for badgers and foxes that dig their dens, or for people like me, who come up here and, when needing protection from the wind, can curl up inside them.

Every time I return to the house where I grew up, here in Italy, between Marche and Umbria, I am eager to live life outdoors. I sleep little and always try to wake up before the sun rises from behind the mountains. I open the bedroom window to let in the scent of the season I find. Sometimes, the smell of lit fireplaces and damp earth wafts in. Other times, the cries of cicadas under the summer heat press against the closed windows, while in the evening, as the coolness descends from the pine forest, the concert is handed over to the crickets, chirping softly before I fall asleep.

For 20 years, I watched this landscape change without truly grasping its value. The seasons shifted, but I was unaware. I cared little for what lay beyond the outline of the mountains I saw in the distance. I knew that where the sun set, there was a mountain called Cucco, round and beautiful like a ring-shaped cake. Looking a bit further to the right, there were two more mountains with feminine names. One called Catria, distant and unreachable, and the other with a mystical and fearsome name, almost meant to deter anyone from venturing there: Monte Strega. Turning my gaze to the east, another stood out: San Vicino, behind whose profile the sun rises in summer. I used to see it with my mother as she drove me to school in the mornings, and I remember the rhyme she recited to me: "If it wears a hat, sell the goats and wear a coat; if San Vicino wears trousers, sell the coat and buy the goats."

The Italian one sounds like:

> "Se mette il cappello, vendi le capre e metti il mantello; se San Vicino mette le brache, vendi il mantello e compra le capre"

(perhaps it sounds better like that).

I never really understood it until recently. If the mountain's summit was covered by clouds or snow like a hat, a long, harsh winter was expected. But if it wore trousers, meaning the fog stayed lower, a milder winter was anticipated. It has been years since San Vicino last wore its hat. In these increasingly mild winters, snowfalls in December are rare and seldom linger long enough to justify "selling the goats and buying the coat."

As the years passed, having to leave this cradle where I grew up, I began to fast from the beauty of this place. When the train left the valley, I entered different landscapes, flat ones, without the contours of the environment I was used to seeing when I opened my windows at home. I began to feel nostalgic for those mountains that for so many years I had considered mere backdrops to my upbringing, but which now embodied a sense of belonging. Over the years and with growing older, I have crossed that passage many times, the one that leads from the Gola della Rossa towards the Vallesina and then to the sea.

At first, heading to the coast, and then further and further away, expanding the frontier of what I considered home. From seeing the world in two dimensions, I transitioned to the three-dimensionality of the environment, piercing the backdrop and seeing beyond what the eye could capture. Beyond the nostalgia I felt (and still feel) when leaving this valley where I grew up, that outline of mountains began to take on the flavor of a frontier towards something new, a symbol of growth and discovery. I remember the effort I made to feel the same connection when looking at the mountains in Switzerland, without success. It was like searching in another woman for the love lost in the past, without allowing the new to enter. They were mountains all the same—magnificent and even more enchanting than those at home. But the sun did not set behind Monte Cucco, nor did it rise from behind San Vicino. The air was not filled with the sound of cicadas when the heat beat down on the fields, nor did it carry the same scent I smelled when I opened my bedroom window.

Now, as I see them from up here, sitting on this plateau, nestled in its hollows, I understand the value of those fasts and how I am not only the child of my parents but also of these places whose names and stories I now know and relive every time I return. The cultivated fields are no longer just a bucolic backdrop in patches; I grasp their functionality, history, and ecological value. The trees marking the boundaries between fields are no longer random interruptions but represent memories of the past. They are homes for sparrows that sing as they build their nests. They are signs that return to the present for those who can notice them, like finding imperfections in ancient paintings that, when observed, serve as windows into the gestures left by the painters.

And yet, this clarity brought by the fast of distance also opens my eyes to other aspects I once overlooked and could not perceive. Being born in this valley is no guarantee of understanding its value. Many have not yet broken through the backdrop of these mountains, even after leaving and returning. The paths see fewer and fewer new footsteps, and those who walk them grow older and older. The gray of abandoned factories clashes with the green surrounding them, and the city itself seems stuck in a stasis, like the days after fairs, when people live in the memory of a happy past among the rolling scraps of paper. I used to think this was unique to the city I lived in, which, having transitioned from a rural past to the wealth of the economic boom, failed to appreciate the environment it was immersed in. Instead, I acknowledge that what happens here is a reflection of what is happening across Italy.

Villages are being abandoned, young people leave, and ecological awareness still resonates little in people's minds, as though it were something that does not concern them. Over the years, people have grown wealthy while impoverishing themselves of the sensitivity to appreciate the beauty they've inherited and to preserve it for those who will come after. They wait for someone else to act and remain blind to what lies ahead. There is blindness to what we have, and we only seem able to appreciate it when it is far away or already lost. Yet, if we broadened our gaze, if we pierced through the backdrop of our lives, we might perceive its depth and understand how, involuntarily, living in nature is the first education in beauty.

## 2. Reflections on the Readings

I wrote these words while sitting at the plateau above home, during one of the walks I take when I return to my parents' place and I treat myself with moments of personal reconnection. As you might have noticed, the reason I decided to add this piece before the reflection is given by the nature the similar themes that emerged during the readings and how they added other lenses to the way I see the environment around me. While walking, I was thinking about some topics we brought up during our classes. Among them, the first I thought about was the sense of belonging one has to the environment where they grew up and how this is revived when walking. I was thinking about the words written by MacFarlane in "The Old Ways".

I believe, above all other readings during the course, this one had the most impact on me. Seeing walking as an act of belonging and, in this case, resistance to the environment where one grew up, is an aspect I had not fully realized before. Probably, it was the way MacFarlane describes walking though sanha ("grazing freely early in the morning like a flock of sheep") that transmitted to me the same sensation I feel every time I give to myself time to walk around the hills of home after seeing the sun rising. Now, even trying to think what it might mean to be forced not to be free to walk around the place where one grew up, it is something I cannot fully grasp it. It goes beyond me, even though it is a reality many face today. The message I carry with me after reading this is that nature knows no politics. There is no political boundary for an acacia, a pine, or a stone, except the one marked by humans. A simple and innocent act such as walking can actually be a deep act of rebellion, reclaiming our rights as living beings on this planet.

A second reading that added a lens to my view was "The Waste Land" by Cal Flyn. Similarly, to the pits left in the meadow where I was sitting next to the trees uprooted by the fire, I thought about how the damage created - radically altering the environment - becomes opportunities that ecology uses to create new niches. Maybe this is not the correct interpretation of the text, but rethinking how residues can become hotspots for biodiversity is something new that I had not considered as much as I should have. Not only that, but seeing how these heaps of waste can take on symbolic value for the community is a thought I had not considered, always clouded by the judgments I made.

The third reading that I felt resonated most deeply with me was "The Politics of the Earth" by John Dryzek. It is likely that the fact I presented it and therefore read it more carefully led me feeling it more than the others. This third reading provided the lens of awareness to what I see. Knowing that a world thought to be unlimited for millennia is just a small blue marble in the universe helps to further downsize the way I see and perceive my surroundings and understand how public opinion reacted before and after this awareness. It often happens when I return home by bike (here in Zürich) that I stop to look at sparrows hiding in the hedges, and I often wonder how they perceive time, pain, and emotions. For them, that flowerbed is the universe. Time flows with a certain rhythm, and their perception of existence and the environment (though we cannot ask them) will likely not be very different from that of many other species of larger dimensions that coexist on this planet (like us humans, for example).

The first message I took from the reading was this: to perceive the boundary of the space we occupy and the limits it entails. Secondly, I was struck by how predictions made 50 years ago with apocalyptic forecasts remain universally unaccepted problems, leaving room for doubt. Learning about different approaches, like "Survivalism" and "Prometheanism", was enriching, and it gave me a new point of view to see and understand the mechanisms that come into play when dealing with wicked problems.

Concluding these reflections, I write as I already mentioned during the lessons, that participating in this course was "cleansing," like cleaning a sieve. I was not able to keep everything I read in my mind. I believe there were even more enriching texts than the ones I mentioned, but I could not retain them as much as I would have liked. However, every time I read, even though it seemed I could not keep the information, I realized that when needed, something from those readings would come back to the surface, and I would see things with greater clarity, noticing aspects I had not before.

I conclude with a feeling I had while sitting in the lessons. During the discussions, sitting in a circle, there was a certain feeling that I had not often experienced before. I knew I could not go wrong by saying what I thought. Whatever the point of view on a reading, sharing it would be equally welcomed and enriching for the discussion. If I ever become a teacher, I think I would offer this same course to students who are willing to join.

Sincerly, thank you
